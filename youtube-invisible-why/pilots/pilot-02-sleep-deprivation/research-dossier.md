# Research Dossier — What 36 Hours Without Sleep Does to Your Decisions

## Central question
What specifically happens inside your brain's decision-making machinery
after roughly a day and a half without sleep — and why does it change
*what* you choose, not just how tired you feel?

## Key findings

1. **Extended wakefulness produces measurable cognitive/motor impairment
   comparable to legal alcohol intoxication thresholds.** Two independent
   lines of research converge on this: after ~17–19 hours awake,
   performance on cognitive/psychomotor tasks was comparable to a blood
   alcohol concentration (BAC) of about 0.05%; by ~24 hours awake,
   comparable to roughly 0.10% BAC (above the legal driving limit in most
   jurisdictions).
   Sources: Dawson, D., & Reid, K. (1997). "Fatigue, alcohol and
   performance impairment." *Nature*, 388(6639), 235. (Tier 1); Williamson,
   A. M., & Feyer, A. M. (2000). "Moderate sleep deprivation produces
   impairments in cognitive and motor performance equivalent to legally
   prescribed levels of alcohol intoxication." *Occupational and
   Environmental Medicine*, 57(10), 649–655. (Tier 1)
   Caveat: these are controlled lab comparisons of task performance curves,
   not a claim that sleep loss and alcohol are biologically identical.

2. **Sleep loss specifically impairs flexible, innovative decision-making
   more than routine tasks.** One night of total sleep loss significantly
   impaired performance on decision-making tasks that required updating
   strategy in response to changing rules — while simpler, well-rehearsed
   tasks were comparatively preserved.
   Source: Harrison, Y., & Horne, J. A. (2000). "One night of sleep loss
   impairs innovative thinking and flexible decision making."
   *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 81(1), 128–145.
   (Tier 1)

3. **Sleep deprivation shifts economic risk preference toward
   risk-seeking**, and this shift is associated with altered activity in
   brain regions involved in valuing outcomes (anterior insula, and
   reduced signaling in regions that normally flag potential losses).
   Source: Venkatraman, V., Chuah, Y. M. L., Huettel, S. A., & Chee, M. W.
   L. (2011). "Sleep deprivation biases the neural mediation of economic
   preferences toward risk seeking." *Journal of Neuroscience*, 31(10),
   3712–3718. (Tier 1)
   Caveat: sample sizes in neuroimaging studies like this are typically
   small (tens of participants); treat the *direction* of the effect
   (toward more risk-seeking) as reasonably well supported, the precise
   neural mechanism as an active research area, not settled fact.

4. **Chronic partial sleep restriction accumulates like debt — and people
   underestimate their own impairment.** Restricting sleep to 4–6 hours a
   night for two weeks produced cognitive deficits equivalent to up to two
   nights of total sleep deprivation, while subjects' own subjective
   sleepiness ratings largely plateaued — they didn't feel as impaired as
   they actually were.
   Source: Van Dongen, H. P. A., Maislin, G., Mullington, J. M., & Dinges,
   D. F. (2003). "The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness:
   dose-response effects on neurobehavioral functions and sleep
   physiology from chronic sleep restriction and total sleep deprivation."
   *Sleep*, 26(2), 117–126. (Tier 1) — one of the most-cited studies in
   sleep research.

5. **General review confirming the pattern across many studies**: sleep
   deprivation reliably impairs judgment, increases risk-taking, and
   increases emotional reactivity, with the prefrontal cortex (responsible
   for planning and impulse control) especially affected.
   Source: Killgore, W. D. S. (2010). "Effects of sleep deprivation on
   cognition." *Progress in Brain Research*, 185, 105–129. (Tier 1 review)

## Deliberately NOT used: "ego depletion" / decision fatigue
Popular coverage of "decision fatigue" often cites Baumeister's ego
depletion research (the idea that willpower is a depletable resource).
A large, preregistered multi-lab replication attempt found no significant
ego depletion effect.
Source: Hagger, M. S., et al. (2016). "A Multilab Preregistered
Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect." *Perspectives on Psychological
Science*, 11(4), 546–573. (Tier 1 replication)
**Editorial decision**: this script grounds its claims in the sleep-
deprivation-specific literature above (which has held up much better),
not in "ego depletion" — per source-policy.md's replication-crisis flag.

## Surprising facts (candidates for the hook/cold open)
- The BAC-equivalence framing (Finding 1) is the strongest cold-open hook:
  most viewers intuitively understand "as impaired as being drunk" far
  faster than a cognitive-test percentage.
- People's *self-rated* sleepiness plateaus even as real performance keeps
  declining (Finding 4) — a strong "you can't trust how awake you feel"
  beat for the turn/deeper-cause section.

## Strongest counter-argument / complicating factor
Individual variation is real — some people (a small minority, sometimes
informally called "short sleepers") tolerate sleep loss better than
others, and effects vary by task type (rote/routine tasks degrade less
than flexible, novel decision-making). The script should avoid implying
everyone is equally and identically impaired at hour 36 — the mechanism is
robust, the magnitude varies.

## Scientific consensus vs. contested
- Consensus: acute and chronic sleep loss impair prefrontal-dependent
  cognition (judgment, flexible decision-making, impulse control) — this
  is one of the most replicated findings in sleep science.
- Consensus: sleep loss shifts choice behavior toward more risk-seeking on
  average.
- Contested/actively researched: the precise neural circuitry, and how
  much of the effect is prefrontal-specific versus broader
  network-level changes.
- Explicitly rejected for this script: ego depletion / "willpower is a
  limited resource" as a general decision-fatigue mechanism (did not
  replicate — see above).

## Possible misinterpretations to guard against
- Don't claim 36 hours is a magic threshold — effects begin accumulating
  well before 36 hours and scale continuously; 36 hours is used as a
  vivid, relatable anchor (a bit more than a full day and night awake),
  not a cliff edge.
- Don't claim sleep loss and alcohol are the "same" biologically — the
  comparison in Finding 1 is about task-performance curves, not shared
  mechanism.
- Don't cite a single exact study's protocol duration ("36 hours") as if
  every finding in this dossier used that exact deprivation length —
  protocols in the cited studies range roughly 17 to 24+ hours acute, or
  chronic partial restriction over weeks; the script should speak
  generally ("the longer you're awake," "by the time you've been up a day
  and a half") rather than attributing every specific number to a 36-hour
  protocol specifically.
