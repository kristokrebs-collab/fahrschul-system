# Script — sleep-deprivation
Target runtime: ~9:55 | Target WPM: 155-165 (voice-style.md)

## [00:00] COLD OPEN
NARRATION: It's 3 AM. You've been awake since yesterday morning. And you're about to make a decision that feels completely reasonable — send the email, buy the thing, say the thing — that you would never make on a normal Tuesday afternoon.
BEAT: A clock is drawn, hands spinning forward. A bed is drawn, empty, off to the side.
NARRATION (cont.): Here's the uncomfortable part: your brain isn't warning you. It feels just as confident as always. That's the problem.
BEAT: A brain is drawn, a checkmark appearing beside it — falsely confident.

## [00:24] PROMISE
NARRATION: By the end of this video, you'll know exactly what's happening inside your skull by hour thirty-six without sleep — and why the danger isn't that you feel terrible. It's that you don't.
BEAT: Text stamps down: "YOU FEEL FINE. THAT'S THE PROBLEM."

## [00:48] SETUP
NARRATION: Let's build the timeline. Hour zero: you wake up like normal. Hours one through sixteen: a completely ordinary day. Nothing unusual yet.
BEAT: A clock is drawn with a slow-moving hand, calm.
NARRATION (cont.): But by hour seventeen or eighteen — late evening, if you started your day at a normal time — something measurable has already changed. Researchers comparing performance on cognitive and motor tasks found that people who'd been awake that long performed about as well as someone with a blood alcohol concentration of roughly zero-point-zero-five percent.
BEAT: A coin-like circular gauge is drawn (reusing the coin icon as a dial), a small marker creeping up it.
NARRATION (cont.): Keep going. By hour twenty-four — a full day awake — that number climbs to somewhere around zero-point-one-zero percent. Above the legal driving limit in most places on Earth.
BEAT: The marker on the dial climbs further, text stamps: "LEGALLY IMPAIRED."

## [02:05] FIRST EXPLANATION
NARRATION: The obvious explanation is: you're tired, so you're slower. Slower reactions, slower typing, slower everything. True — but it misses the part that actually matters.
BEAT: A phone icon is drawn, then an arrow-down beside it, gently.
NARRATION (cont.): Being slow isn't what gets people into trouble at 3 AM. Feeling completely fine while being fundamentally wrong is what gets people into trouble.
BEAT: The brain icon reappears with a checkmark next to it, unmoving, confident.

## [02:55] TURN
NARRATION: Here's the twist researchers actually found: when they measured how sleepy people said they felt, night after night of restricted sleep, those ratings leveled off. People stopped reporting feeling worse.
BEAT: A calendar is drawn, with a flat line drawn across several days.
NARRATION (cont.): But their actual performance on real tasks kept getting worse. The gap between how impaired people felt and how impaired they actually were just kept widening — night after night — and almost nobody noticed the gap opening.
BEAT: A second line — a downward arrow — is drawn beneath the flat calendar line, the space between them growing.

## [04:00] TURN (continued)
NARRATION: So your brain isn't lying to you exactly. It's just using an outdated instrument — your own sense of "I feel okay" — to make a call it's no longer qualified to make.
BEAT: The brain icon is redrawn with the confident checkmark, camera zooms in tight on the mismatch between brain and checkmark.
NARRATION (cont.): And it's not just slower thinking. Researchers scanning brains during decision-making tasks found that after sleep loss, people shift toward riskier choices — and you can see it happening in brain regions that are supposed to flag "hey, this could go badly."
BEAT: An eye icon is drawn, then fades, as an arrow-up icon appears representing "risk climbing."

## [05:15] DEEPER CAUSE
NARRATION: Here's the part almost nobody explains well: this isn't about willpower running out, like a battery draining. That old idea — that you have a limited pool of self-control that gets used up over the day — hasn't held up well when other scientists tried to replicate it at scale.
BEAT: A battery icon is drawn, then a large x-mark is drawn over it.
NARRATION (cont.): What's actually happening is more specific, and more physical: the parts of your brain responsible for flexible planning and impulse control depend on sleep to function properly. Take the sleep away, and that specific system — not your character, not your intelligence — degrades on schedule.
BEAT: Brain icon redrawn, a small highlighted region drawn within it (front area), camera slow zoom in.

## [06:30] DEEPER CAUSE (continued)
NARRATION: And it hits flexible decisions harder than routine ones. In one study, one night without sleep barely touched people's ability to do simple, well-rehearsed tasks — but it clearly damaged their ability to update a plan when the rules changed.
BEAT: Two paths are drawn — a straight arrow (routine) stays crisp; a branching path (flexible decision) is drawn shakily.
NARRATION (cont.): Which is exactly the kind of decision most late-night, high-stakes choices actually are. Not routine. Novel. Something you haven't decided a hundred times before, at the exact moment your brain is worst equipped to decide it well.
BEAT: Camera zooms out to reveal both paths together on the page.

## [07:45] PAYOFF SETUP
NARRATION: So what do you actually do with this? Not "try to feel less tired" — you've already seen that feeling isn't a reliable gauge anymore by this point.
BEAT: Clock redrawn, a lightbulb appears beside it.
NARRATION (cont.): The fix is structural, not motivational: treat hour seventeen and beyond as a different decision-making mode, the way you'd treat driving on ice differently from driving on a dry road — not because you feel different, but because you know the conditions changed.
BEAT: An arrow-down draws downward into a wall — representing a decision hitting a barrier — then a checkmark redirects it.

## [08:35] PAYOFF
NARRATION: Concretely: big decisions — money, relationships, anything you can't easily undo — get a rule. Not after a full night without sleep. Sleep first, decide after. If it still feels urgent in the morning, it was probably actually urgent.
BEAT: A calendar is drawn with a checkmark stamped on the next morning's date.
NARRATION (cont.): And if you manage people, or make decisions that affect others, this is worth saying out loud to your team, too: "ask me again after I've slept" isn't weakness. It's just accurate risk management for a brain that, by hour thirty-six, is quietly running on borrowed judgment.
BEAT: A person icon is drawn beside the brain, both calm now.
NARRATION (cont.): You don't feel impaired at 3 AM. That's exactly why it's worth having the rule before you need it.
BEAT: Final wide shot — camera zooms out to reveal clock, bed, brain, calendar, and person all together on the page — accent-colored underline draws beneath the closing frame.
