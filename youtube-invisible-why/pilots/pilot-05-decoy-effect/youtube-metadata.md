## Description
```
There's a price on almost every pricing page that exists only to make you
pick a different one. It isn't there to be bought. It's there to be
rejected — and once you can see it, you can't stop seeing it.

This video walks through the cleanest demonstration of it ever run: a real
Economist subscription offer with one option nobody chose, and what
happened to sales when that useless option was deleted. The answer is not
"nothing."

It also covers where the effect is weaker than the internet claims, because
a three-tier pricing page is not automatically a trick.

CHAPTERS
0:00 The price nobody is meant to buy
0:20 The offer that looked like a mistake
1:05 Delete the option nobody wanted
1:40 Why a comparison beats a judgement
2:15 How to build one on purpose
2:50 Where this gets overstated
3:15 The defence

SOURCES
• Ariely, D. (2008). Predictably Irrational, ch. 1 — The Economist
  subscription experiment: 16/0/84 with the decoy, 68/32 without it.
• Huber, J., Payne, J. W., & Puto, C. (1982). "Adding Asymmetrically
  Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity
  Hypothesis." Journal of Consumer Research, 9(1), 90–98 — the original
  finding.
• Frederick, S., Lee, L., & Baskin, E. (2014). "The Limits of Attraction."
  Journal of Marketing Research, 51(4), 487–507 — replication difficulties
  with real, tangible products. This is why the video does not claim the
  effect works on everyone, every time.

This video explains a mechanism. It is not purchasing advice.
```
