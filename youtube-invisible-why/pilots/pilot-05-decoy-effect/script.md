# Script — decoy-effect
Target runtime: ~5:10 | Target WPM: 150–160 | speech_rate: 20

Structure note: seven beats, each closing a loop and opening the next.

## [00:00] COLD OPEN — 12s
NARRATION: There's a price on this page that exists only to make you pick a different one. It isn't there to be bought. It's there to be rejected.
BEAT: Three price boxes draw in fast. The left one immediately greys out and shrinks — not chosen, but not gone.
OPEN LOOP: Which one is the fake, and why would anyone sell it?

## [00:12] PROMISE — 7s
NARRATION: Once you can see it, you can't stop seeing it. It's in almost every pricing page you've ever looked at.
BEAT: Text "THE DECOY" stamps down hard.
OPEN LOOP: How does a price you don't buy change the one you do?

## [00:19] SETUP — 44s
NARRATION: In two thousand eight, the behavioural economist Dan Ariely found a subscription offer from The Economist and thought it looked like a mistake. Three options. Web only, fifty-nine dollars. Print only, one hundred and twenty-five. Print *and* web — also one hundred and twenty-five dollars.
BEAT: Three boxes draw with prices landing one at a time, hard stamps. The two 125s sit side by side.
NARRATION (cont.): Read that again. Print alone cost exactly the same as print plus web. Nobody would choose print alone. It's strictly worse for the identical price.
BEAT: The middle box gets a thick X drawn through it. Zero.
NARRATION (cont.): So Ariely ran it on a hundred students. Sixteen chose web only. Zero chose print only. Eighty-four chose the combo. Then he deleted the useless option and ran it again on a fresh hundred.
BEAT: Bar chart draws: 16 / 0 / 84. Then the middle bar is erased and the chart holds, empty, waiting.
OPEN LOOP: What happens when you remove an option nobody picked?

## [01:03] TURN — 40s
NARRATION: Logically, nothing should change. Nobody wanted it. Removing it should just leave the same two choices and the same split.
BEAT: The two remaining boxes sit alone. A question mark hovers.
NARRATION (cont.): The result flipped. Without the decoy, sixty-eight chose web only and just thirty-two chose the combo. The expensive option lost more than half its buyers — because a product nobody bought stopped standing next to it.
BEAT: HARD CUT — pattern interrupt. New bar chart slams in over the old one: 68 / 32. The two charts sit stacked, impossible to misread.
OPEN LOOP: Why does an option nobody chooses have that much power?

## [01:43] DEEPER CAUSE — 50s
NARRATION: Because you are very bad at judging what something is worth, and very good at judging which of two things is better.
BEAT: A single price floats alone with a question mark. Then a second price appears next to it — instantly, a checkmark lands on one.
NARRATION (cont.): Ask someone what a hundred and twenty-five dollars of journalism is worth and they have no idea. There's no internal price list. But put print-only next to print-plus-web and the comparison is trivial: same money, more stuff. Obviously better.
BEAT: Two boxes, a bright accent arrow snapping from one to the other. Easy. Fast.
NARRATION (cont.): That comparison feels like a judgement about value. It isn't. It's a judgement about *those two options*. And someone chose which two options you'd be comparing.
BEAT: Camera pulls back — a hand is placing the boxes on the page, arranging them deliberately.
OPEN LOOP: So how do you build one on purpose?

## [02:33] MECHANISM — 42s
NARRATION: The recipe is precise. You need a target — the thing you want sold. Then you add a decoy that is clearly worse than the target, but *not* clearly worse than the third option.
BEAT: Three boxes label themselves: TARGET, DECOY, ALTERNATIVE. The decoy sits visibly beneath the target.
NARRATION (cont.): The decoy's whole job is to be dominated by exactly one option. It makes the target look like a win, and a win is much easier to choose than a value judgement.
BEAT: Decoy box dims. An arrow from decoy to target lights up in accent. The third box sits untouched, ignored.
NARRATION (cont.): Popcorn sizes work this way. Phone storage tiers work this way. So does almost every three-tier software page where the middle plan is mysteriously the best deal.
BEAT: Rapid montage — popcorn cups, storage tiers, pricing cards. Same three-box skeleton under each, drawn in accent.
OPEN LOOP: Is this manipulation, or just marketing?

## [03:15] FAIRNESS BEAT — 36s
NARRATION: Worth being careful here. A three-tier pricing page is not automatically a trick. Sometimes the middle plan genuinely is the best fit for most people, and saying so is helpful, not manipulative.
BEAT: Two paths split — one to a checkmark, one to an X, both from the same three boxes.
NARRATION (cont.): And the effect is smaller in the wild than in the lab. Real buyers have budgets, brand loyalty, and a habit of leaving the page entirely. The Economist study is a clean demonstration of a real mechanism — not a promise that it works on everyone, every time.
BEAT: The bar chart from earlier redraws, then softens — edges blurring slightly, honest about its own limits.
OPEN LOOP: Then what do you actually do about it?

## [03:51] PAYOFF — 44s
NARRATION: The defence is almost annoyingly simple, because the effect only works while all the options sit next to each other.
BEAT: Three boxes. A hand covers two of them with a sheet of paper.
NARRATION (cont.): Before you look at the tiers, decide what you actually need. Write the number down if you have to. Then look. The decoy can only move you if you arrive without a position, because it works by supplying one.
BEAT: A number is written on paper *first*. Then the boxes are revealed. The accent arrow tries to snap toward the target — and doesn't reach.
NARRATION (cont.): And when one option is transparently worse than another at the same price, that's not a bad deal you're being offered. That's a signpost, pointing at the option someone would like you to take.
BEAT: The decoy box redraws — and a small arrow-shaped signpost is drawn inside it, pointing at the target.
NARRATION (cont.): You're not being fooled about value. You're being handed a comparison, and comparisons are the one thing your brain never refuses.
BEAT: Final wide shot — camera zooms out: all three boxes, the two charts, the hand, the signpost, one connected page. Accent underline draws beneath.
CLOSE: Loop closes.

## Sources used inline
- Ariely, D. (2008). *Predictably Irrational*, ch. 1 — The Economist
  subscription experiment, 100 MIT students, 16/0/84 → 68/32
- Huber, J., Payne, J. W., & Puto, C. (1982). "Adding Asymmetrically
  Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity
  Hypothesis." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 9(1), 90–98 — the original
  asymmetric dominance / decoy finding
- Frederick, S., Lee, L., & Baskin, E. (2014). "The Limits of Attraction."
  *Journal of Marketing Research*, 51(4) — replication difficulties with
  real products; the basis for the fairness beat. Do not cut this beat.
