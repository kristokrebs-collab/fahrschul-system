# Research Dossier — Why Your Brain Refuses to Cancel Subscriptions

## Central question
Why do people keep paying for subscriptions they don't use, even when they
consciously intend to cancel — and how much of that is psychology versus
how much is the product being deliberately built that way?

## Key findings

1. **Status quo bias**: people disproportionately stick with a default or
   current state, even when switching would clearly benefit them, because
   the reference point for gains/losses is the status quo itself.
   Source: Samuelson, W., & Zeckhauser, R. (1988). "Status Quo Bias in
   Decision Making." *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*, 1(1), 7–59. (Tier 1)
   Caveat: original work is theoretical/experimental with choice
   scenarios, not a subscription-specific field study — the general
   mechanism is well established, but the magnitude in a subscription
   context specifically comes from Finding 3 below.

2. **Loss aversion / the endowment effect**: losses loom larger than
   equivalent gains, so "losing access" to a service you already have
   feels worse than the (equal-sized) gain of not paying for it would feel
   good — which nudges people toward inaction.
   Source: Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1991).
   "Anomalies: The Endowment Effect, Loss Aversion, and Status Quo Bias."
   *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 5(1), 193–206. (Tier 1)

3. **Sunk cost fallacy**: people continue an ongoing commitment in part
   because of resources (money, time, a "yearly plan") already invested,
   even though a rational actor should only weigh future costs/benefits.
   Source: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). "The Psychology of Sunk
   Cost." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 35(1),
   124–140. (Tier 1)

4. **Cancellation friction is often deliberately engineered, not
   incidental.** A large-scale automated audit of shopping/subscription
   sites found widespread "dark patterns," including the "Roach Motel"
   pattern — easy to get in, deliberately hard to get out (requiring phone
   calls, multi-step flows, retention offers, or hidden cancellation
   pages).
   Source: Mathur, A., Kshirsagar, M., & Mayer, J. (2021). "What Makes a
   Dark Pattern... Dark? Design Attributes, Normative Considerations, and
   Measurement Methods." *Proceedings of the 2021 CHI Conference on Human
   Factors in Computing Systems.* Related earlier crawl: Mathur, Mayer,
   et al. (2019) "Dark Patterns at Scale: Findings from a Crawl of 11K
   Shopping Websites." (Tier 1 — peer-reviewed, large-scale empirical
   measurement)

5. **Regulators have explicitly responded to this pattern.** The U.S.
   Federal Trade Commission finalized amendments to its Negative Option
   Rule in 2024 (the "click-to-cancel" rule), which would require
   cancellation to be at least as easy as sign-up.
   Source: Federal Trade Commission, "Negative Option Rule" final rule,
   16 CFR Part 425 (2024). (Tier 2 — regulatory primary source)
   Caveat: the rule has faced legal challenges since finalization and its
   enforcement status may have changed by the time this video is published
   — state this as "regulators moved to require X" rather than "it is now
   the law everywhere," and verify current status before publishing.

## Surprising facts (candidates for the hook/cold open)
- The average number of forgotten/unused subscriptions per person is
  frequently cited in industry surveys (e.g., by subscription-management
  apps) in the range of 2–4 — these are **Tier 3 industry surveys, not
  peer-reviewed**, so the script should describe this qualitatively
  ("most people are paying for something they forgot about") rather than
  quoting a precise number as fact.
- Some services' cancel flows are measurably longer (more clicks/steps)
  than their signup flows — this is exactly what the dark-patterns
  research in Finding 4 measured directly.

## Strongest counter-argument / complicating factor
Not all cancellation friction is manipulative — some of it is retention
teams genuinely trying to offer a discount or fix the reason someone wants
to leave, and some "friction" is just normal customer-service load
(needing to verify identity, prevent frauduluent cancellations, etc.). The
script should distinguish "friction that protects the user" from
"friction that exploits the biases in Findings 1–3," rather than treating
all friction as malicious.

## Scientific consensus vs. contested
- Consensus: status quo bias, loss aversion, and sunk cost effects are
  robust, widely replicated phenomena in behavioral economics.
- Consensus: dark-pattern cancellation friction is empirically measured
  and documented at scale, not merely anecdotal.
- Contested/uncertain: the *exact* average dollar amount lost per consumer
  per year to forgotten subscriptions — industry figures vary widely and
  are marketing-adjacent (Tier 3), so the script avoids citing a single
  precise dollar figure as settled fact.

## Possible misinterpretations to guard against
- Don't imply every subscription company is acting in bad faith — many
  aren't; frame it as "some services exploit well-documented biases,"
  not "all subscriptions are scams."
- Don't overstate FTC rule's current enforceability without a status check
  at time of publishing (see caveat in Finding 5).
