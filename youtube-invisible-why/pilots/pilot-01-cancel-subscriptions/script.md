# Script — cancel-subscriptions
Target runtime: ~9:50 | Target WPM: 155-165 (voice-style.md)

## [00:00] COLD OPEN
NARRATION: Somewhere on your card statement right now, there's a charge you forgot you agreed to. Not a scam. Not a mistake. Something you signed up for on purpose, meant to cancel, and just... didn't.
BEAT: A credit card is drawn. A small looping icon appears next to a line item, pulsing quietly.

## [00:22] PROMISE
NARRATION: That's not a willpower problem. It's not because you're lazy or bad with money. By the end of this video, you'll know exactly which parts of your brain a cancel button is designed to fight — and win.
BEAT: Text reveals "BY DESIGN" briefly, then fades as the frame widens.

## [00:45] SETUP
NARRATION: Picture the moment you signed up. It took maybe ninety seconds. One page, one button, done. Now picture the last time you tried to cancel something. A settings menu that doesn't have a "cancel" option — just "pause," or "downgrade," or a suspiciously cheerful "are you sure?" screen. Maybe a phone number you had to call, during business hours, to talk a real person out of letting you leave.
BEAT: A phone is drawn, then a maze-like wall with a small door.
NARRATION (cont.): You're not imagining that asymmetry. Researchers who studied thousands of shopping and subscription sites found that a meaningful share of them use exactly this pattern on purpose — sign-up in one click, cancellation in five, ten, sometimes requiring a phone call. It even has a name in the research: the "Roach Motel." Easy in. Hard out.
BEAT: On-screen text "EASY IN. HARD OUT." draws over the wall/door illustration.

## [01:35] FIRST EXPLANATION
NARRATION: The obvious explanation is: companies want your money, so they make leaving annoying. True, but incomplete — because friction alone doesn't explain why you don't cancel even when nothing is stopping you. Plenty of subscriptions you could kill in two taps, right now, and still won't.
BEAT: A calendar is drawn with several small recurring-charge icons scattered across it.
NARRATION (cont.): So there's something happening before you even reach the cancel button. Something that decides, quietly, that today isn't the day.
BEAT: A brain is drawn, faint question mark hovering beside it.

## [02:25] TURN
NARRATION: Here's the first piece. Economists call it status quo bias — a well-documented tendency to stick with whatever is already happening, even when switching would clearly be better for you. Not because switching is hard. Just because it's not the current state.
BEAT: An arrow-up icon representing "current plan" stays fixed at center while smaller options fade in and out around it, none of them chosen.
NARRATION (cont.): Pair that with something called loss aversion: losing something you already have feels worse, psychologically, than an equal-sized gain feels good. So when your brain weighs "cancel and save twelve dollars" against "lose access to something I already own" — even something you never use — the losing side gets weighted more heavily. It's not a fair fight, and it isn't supposed to be one.
BEAT: A simple scale-like layout — coin icon on one side, x-mark on the other — tips toward the x-mark, then camera zooms out to reveal both sides at once.

## [03:35] TURN (continued)
NARRATION: Then there's the money you already spent. If you paid for the annual plan to get a discount, cancelling in month four doesn't just mean stopping future charges — it means admitting the yearly payment was, in part, wasted. That's the sunk cost fallacy: weighing money you can never get back as if it were still a reason to keep going.
BEAT: A coin icon is drawn, then a calendar with months ticking by, the coin fading slightly with each tick.
NARRATION (cont.): None of these are new tricks. Behavioral economists have been mapping them for decades. What's new is that modern subscription products are the first products in history precisely engineered, tested, and optimized against exactly these biases — at scale, on millions of people, continuously.
BEAT: Camera pulls back further — "zoom out to reveal" — showing the credit card, calendar, brain, and scale all connected on the same page.

## [04:40] DEEPER CAUSE
NARRATION: So who's actually deciding you should stay? Not one villain. A retention team, running experiments most users never notice. They test which exit screens make people change their minds. They test how many steps a cancellation flow can have before people give up partway through. They test which discount offer, shown at exactly the right moment of doubt, gets you to click "keep my plan" instead.
BEAT: A person icon is drawn, small, standing in front of a much larger wall structure with several small doors.
NARRATION (cont.): This isn't a conspiracy theory — it's closer to a job description. And it works precisely because it's not fighting your logic. It's fighting your defaults, your aversion to loss, and your reluctance to "waste" what you already paid. Three biases that exist in every human brain, whether or not you've ever heard their names.
BEAT: Brain icon redrawn, with the three earlier icons (arrow-up, coin, x-mark) orbiting it briefly before settling.

## [06:00] DEEPER CAUSE (continued)
NARRATION: It's worth being fair here: not all of this is malicious. Some of that friction is genuinely there to stop fraud, verify it's really you, or — sometimes — to make a real offer that actually helps. A retention discount isn't automatically a trick. The problem isn't that companies want to keep customers. The problem is when the flow is built to exploit a bias instead of to earn a decision.
BEAT: Two paths diverge on screen — one leads to a checkmark, one to an x-mark, both starting from the same wall.
NARRATION (cont.): Regulators have started drawing that line too. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission tried to make cancelling as easy as signing up — a court sent the rule back over a paperwork technicality, and regulators are now redoing it properly. Whatever happens to that specific rule, the fact that regulators keep trying tells you the pattern was real enough, and common enough, to legislate against.
BEAT: A simple courthouse-like wall icon appears briefly, then fades, camera slow zoom out.

## [07:40] PAYOFF SETUP
NARRATION: So what actually works, if the trick is aimed at your defaults, not your intelligence? Not "try harder to remember." You already know you should cancel that one thing. Remembering isn't the problem.
BEAT: Phone icon redrawn, small lightbulb appears beside it.
NARRATION (cont.): What works is changing what the default is. Put every subscription on a calendar the day you sign up, with a reminder before the renewal — so cancelling becomes the thing that happens automatically unless you actively choose to keep paying. That flips status quo bias to work for you instead of against you.
BEAT: Calendar redrawn with a checkmark stamped near a renewal date.

## [08:35] PAYOFF
NARRATION: And when you do hit a cancellation flow that fights you — multiple screens, a guilt-trip pop-up, a hidden phone number — that's useful information. It's not you being difficult. It's the company confirming, in the design of its own product, that it doesn't expect to win you back on value alone.
BEAT: Wall icon with the small door redrawn, and a checkmark draws itself through the door.
NARRATION (cont.): You're not bad at money. You're a person with a normal, well-documented brain, sitting across from a product built by people whose job is to out-think exactly that brain. Now you know which parts they're aiming at. That's most of the fight, right there.
BEAT: Final wide shot — camera zooms out to reveal the full page: card, calendar, brain, scale, wall, all drawn together — then the accent-colored underline draws beneath the channel's closing frame.
