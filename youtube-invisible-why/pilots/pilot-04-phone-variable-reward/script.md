# Script — phone-variable-reward
Target runtime: ~5:40 | Target WPM: 150–160 | speech_rate: 20

Structure note: seven beats, each one closing a loop and opening the next.
No beat ends on a summary. See `channel-bible/retention-playbook.md` §2.

## [00:00] COLD OPEN — 13s
NARRATION: You just picked up your phone. You don't remember deciding to. Your hand moved, the screen lit up, and somewhere in that half-second, a decision got made without you.
BEAT: Hand enters frame holding nothing. Phone materializes in it. Rewind-scrub back — the hand is empty again. Loop twice, faster.
OPEN LOOP: What made the decision, if you didn't?

## [00:13] PROMISE — 8s
NARRATION: That gap — between your hand moving and you noticing — is engineered. And the blueprint is eighty years old.
BEAT: Text "1948" stamps hard onto paper. Everything else drops away.
OPEN LOOP: What happened in 1948?

## [00:21] SETUP — 42s
NARRATION: In the nineteen-forties, a psychologist named B.F. Skinner put pigeons in boxes with a lever. Press the lever, get food. The pigeons learned fast, pressed when hungry, stopped when full. Predictable. Boring.
BEAT: Box drawn. Lever. Pigeon. Food pellet drops on a steady rhythm — tick, tick, tick. Metronome-regular.
NARRATION (cont.): Then he changed one thing. The food stopped coming every time. Now it came sometimes. Randomly. The pigeon couldn't tell which press would pay.
BEAT: The steady rhythm breaks. Pellets drop irregularly — gap, gap, drop, gap, drop drop, long gap.
NARRATION (cont.): The pigeons didn't press less. They pressed more. A lot more. Some kept pressing thousands of times after the food stopped entirely.
BEAT: Counter spins up: 12 → 340 → 2,800. The pigeon silhouette blurs with speed.
OPEN LOOP: Why does *less* reward produce *more* behavior?

## [01:03] FIRST EXPLANATION — 38s
NARRATION: The obvious answer is that the pigeon is confused. It isn't. It's doing something rational under bad information.
BEAT: Two paths split — "CONFUSED" crossed out, "RATIONAL" underlined in accent.
NARRATION (cont.): When a reward is reliable, you learn when to stop. Full stomach, stop pressing. But when a reward is random, there's no signal that says "not now." Every press is a fresh coin flip. And the only way to lose the flip you might have won is to stop flipping.
BEAT: Coin flips across the page. Some land accent-side up, most don't. The losing ones fade; the winning ones stay bright.
OPEN LOOP: So where did this leave the lab?

## [01:41] TURN — 46s
NARRATION: Skinner called it a variable ratio schedule. It is the most persistent pattern of behavior psychology has ever produced in a laboratory. And it did not stay in the laboratory.
BEAT: Pigeon box redraws — and morphs. Lever becomes a slot machine handle. Same shape, different object.
NARRATION (cont.): Slot machines run on it. That's not a metaphor — it's the actual design principle, and it's why a slot machine pays out constantly in small amounts instead of rarely in large ones.
BEAT: Slot machine. Reels spin. Small payout. Small payout. Nothing. Nothing. Small payout.
NARRATION (cont.): Now look at the gesture you make to refresh a feed. Pull down. Release. Wait for the reels to stop.
BEAT: HARD CUT — pattern interrupt. Slot machine handle and pull-to-refresh gesture drawn side by side, same arc, same motion.
OPEN LOOP: If the mechanism is identical, is the intent?

## [02:27] DEEPER CAUSE — 52s
NARRATION: Here's where it gets more careful than the usual version of this story. Nobody sat in a meeting and said "let's build a slot machine." They didn't have to.
BEAT: Meeting room drawn, then erased — the hand crosses it out.
NARRATION (cont.): What actually happened is that thousands of design variations got tested against a single number: how often people come back. Variations that produced unpredictable rewards kept people coming back more. So those variations survived. Nobody needed to know why.
BEAT: Grid of small interface variants. Most fade out. A few brighten and multiply. Evolutionary selection, visualized.
NARRATION (cont.): That's the uncomfortable part. This isn't a conspiracy that requires villains. It's a selection process that produces the same result as a conspiracy, and it runs whether or not anyone understands the psychology underneath.
BEAT: Camera pulls back — the surviving variants form the shape of the original Skinner box.
OPEN LOOP: Does knowing this change anything?

## [03:19] FAIRNESS BEAT — 34s
NARRATION: It's worth saying what this doesn't mean. Your phone isn't a slot machine, and you aren't a pigeon. Most of what arrives on it is genuinely wanted — messages from people you love, work you're waiting on, news you asked for.
BEAT: Phone redrawn. Real, warm content fills it — a message, a photo, a call.
NARRATION (cont.): And the popular claim that every notification gives you a dopamine hit oversimplifies what dopamine actually does. The honest version is narrower and, I think, more interesting: dopamine tracks *anticipation* more than reward. It spikes before you look, not after.
BEAT: Graph draws. The spike lands clearly *before* the "reward" marker, not on it.
OPEN LOOP: So what is the reaching actually for?

## [03:53] PAYOFF SETUP — 40s
NARRATION: That's the thing to sit with. The pull isn't the reward. The pull is the *maybe*.
BEAT: Text "THE MAYBE" draws large and stays. Everything else dims.
NARRATION (cont.): Which explains something you've probably noticed and found strange: you check, there's nothing there, you feel briefly flat — and then you check again nine minutes later. If the reward were the point, an empty check would teach you to stop. It doesn't, because the empty check isn't a failure of the system. It's the system working.
BEAT: Timeline of checks across an hour. Most are empty. The empty ones pulse, not the full ones.
OPEN LOOP: Then what actually works?

## [04:33] PAYOFF — 48s
NARRATION: If the mechanism runs on unpredictability, then willpower is the wrong tool. You can't out-discipline a coin flip — you can only stop flipping.
BEAT: Hand pushes the coin off the edge of the page.
NARRATION (cont.): What works is making the reward predictable again. Not "use your phone less" — that's the pigeon pressing harder. Instead: check on a schedule you choose, not on a feeling. Turn off the notifications that arrive at random and keep the ones that arrive for a reason. Both of those do the same thing — they take the randomness out, and randomness was the whole engine.
BEAT: The irregular pellet rhythm from the opening redraws — then snaps back to the steady metronome tick from before Skinner's change.
NARRATION (cont.): You're not weak. You're running correct software on a problem it was never built for, against a system that found the exploit by accident and kept it because it worked.
BEAT: Final wide shot. Camera zooms out: box, lever, coin, phone, graph — all one connected drawing. Accent underline draws beneath.
CLOSE: No question. The loop closes here.

## Sources used inline
- Skinner, B. F. (1957). *Schedules of Reinforcement* (with C. B. Ferster) —
  variable ratio schedules producing highest, most persistent response rates
- Berridge, K. C., & Robinson, T. E. (1998). "What is the role of dopamine in
  reward: hedonic impact, reward learning, or incentive salience?" — the
  wanting/liking distinction that makes the "dopamine hit" framing wrong
- Schultz, W. (1998). "Predictive reward signal of dopamine neurons" —
  dopamine responds to prediction error, spikes on anticipation
- Note: the "apps are literally slot machines" claim is framed here as
  mechanism-identical, intent-unproven. Do not strengthen it. See
  `editorial-rules.md` §2.
