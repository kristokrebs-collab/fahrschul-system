## Description
```
Your hand moves before you notice. The screen is already on. Somewhere in
that half-second a decision got made, and you weren't the one who made it.

That gap is engineered — and the blueprint is eighty years old. This video
traces it from a pigeon in a box in the nineteen-forties to the gesture you
make to refresh a feed, and explains why the empty check is not the system
failing. It's the system working.

It also corrects the version of this story you've probably heard: the
"every notification is a dopamine hit" line oversimplifies what dopamine
does. The real finding is narrower, and more interesting.

CHAPTERS
0:00 The decision you didn't make
0:22 A pigeon, a lever, and one change
1:10 Why less reward produces more behaviour
1:45 The most persistent pattern in psychology
2:20 Nobody had to design this
2:55 What this doesn't mean
3:20 The pull is the maybe

SOURCES
• Ferster, C. B., & Skinner, B. F. (1957). Schedules of Reinforcement —
  variable ratio schedules produce the highest, most persistent response
  rates of any reinforcement schedule.
• Berridge, K. C., & Robinson, T. E. (1998). "What is the role of dopamine
  in reward: hedonic impact, reward learning, or incentive salience?"
  Brain Research Reviews, 28(3), 309–369 — the wanting/liking distinction.
• Schultz, W. (1998). "Predictive Reward Signal of Dopamine Neurons."
  Journal of Neurophysiology, 80(1), 1–27 — dopamine responds to prediction
  error and spikes on anticipation rather than on receipt.

This video explains a mechanism. The claim that apps are deliberately built
as slot machines is presented as mechanism-identical and intent-unproven,
which is where the evidence actually sits.
```
