Why Waiting Feels Longer Than It Is

## Description
```
An airport kept getting complaints about baggage delays. It hired more
staff. Bags came out faster. The complaints didn't stop.

So it tried something that shouldn't have worked: it made the walk longer.
Complaints dropped to near zero — with no extra staff, no new equipment,
and a total journey that was the same or slightly worse.

This video explains why, using the one distinction that makes waiting make
sense: you don't experience a wait as a length of time. You experience it
as a length of attention. It also draws the line between designing a wait
that respects you and one that disguises itself.

CHAPTERS
0:00 The fix that made the wait longer
0:20 Time versus attention
1:00 Why some waits feel unfair
1:35 What the airport actually did
2:10 Complaints to zero
2:35 Manipulation, or design?

SOURCES
• Stone, A. (2012). "Why Waiting Is Torture." The New York Times, Aug 18 —
  the Houston gate-relocation account (roughly 1 min walk / 7 min wait
  becoming 6 min walk / 2 min wait, complaints to near zero). Reported
  figures, not a controlled study — the video frames it that way.
• Maister, D. H. (1985). "The Psychology of Waiting Lines" — occupied
  versus unoccupied time; unexplained and uncertain waits feel longer;
  unfair waits feel longest.
• Larson, R. C. (1987). "Perspectives on Queues: Social Justice and the
  Psychology of Queueing." Operations Research, 35(6), 895–905 — queue
  fairness and the single-serpentine-line result.
```
