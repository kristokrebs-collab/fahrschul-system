# Script — waiting-time
Target runtime: ~5:00 | Target WPM: 150–160 | speech_rate: 20

Structure note: seven beats, each closing a loop and opening the next.
The Houston airport story is the payoff — do not spend it early.

## [00:00] COLD OPEN — 14s
NARRATION: An airport got so many complaints about baggage delays that it hired more staff. Bags came out faster. The complaints didn't stop. So they tried something that shouldn't have worked.
BEAT: A baggage carousel draws. A complaint counter ticks up fast. Staff icons are added — the counter keeps climbing.
OPEN LOOP: What do you do when fixing the problem doesn't fix the complaint?

## [00:14] PROMISE — 7s
NARRATION: The fix took no extra staff, no new equipment, and made the wait objectively longer.
BEAT: Text "LONGER" stamps down. Then, beneath it, smaller: "and better".
OPEN LOOP: How is a longer wait a better wait?

## [00:21] SETUP — 40s
NARRATION: Start with the thing everyone gets wrong about waiting. You don't experience a wait as a length of time. You experience it as a length of *attention*.
BEAT: Two identical bars draw side by side, same length. One fills with small ticking marks; the other stays blank.
NARRATION (cont.): Researchers have measured this repeatedly. People asked to wait with nothing to do consistently overestimate how long they waited — often by a lot. Give them something to occupy attention and the same wait shrinks.
BEAT: The blank bar visibly stretches longer while the occupied bar stays put. Same clock underneath both, unchanged.
NARRATION (cont.): This is why the wait for a lift feels endless and the walk to a further lift doesn't, even when the walk takes more of your life.
BEAT: Two floor plans, side by side, timed identically. The waiting one glows red; the walking one stays calm.
OPEN LOOP: If empty time is the problem, what fills it?

## [01:01] TURN — 42s
NARRATION: There's a second layer, and it's the reason a wait can feel unfair rather than just long.
BEAT: A queue of figures draws. One figure at the back.
NARRATION (cont.): Waits feel worse when you can't see the end, when you don't know why you're waiting, and — most powerfully — when someone who arrived after you gets served first.
BEAT: Three quick vignettes, hard cuts: a queue vanishing round a corner; a blank departure board; a figure cutting the line, drawn in harsh accent.
NARRATION (cont.): That last one is why a single winding queue feeding several tills feels fairer than separate lines, even though the average wait is the same. The single queue removes the possibility of picking wrong.
BEAT: HARD CUT — pattern interrupt. Parallel queues morph into one serpentine queue, the lines physically sliding together.
OPEN LOOP: So back to the airport. What did they actually do?

## [01:43] DEEPER CAUSE — 46s
NARRATION: The airport looked at the numbers and found something strange. Passengers walked about one minute from the gate to baggage claim, then stood at the carousel for about seven.
BEAT: A corridor draws short. Then the standing time draws long — a figure rooted in place while a counter climbs.
NARRATION (cont.): Roughly eight minutes total. But only one of those minutes was occupied. Seven were spent doing nothing but waiting, which is exactly the kind of time that stretches.
BEAT: The eight-minute bar splits: a thin occupied slice, a huge empty one. The empty part visibly expands.
NARRATION (cont.): So they moved the arrival gates further from the terminal, and routed the bags to the most distant carousel. Passengers now walked about six minutes and waited about two.
BEAT: The corridor redraws — much longer. The standing figure now walks. The waiting slice shrinks.
OPEN LOOP: Same total time. Did anything actually change?

## [02:29] PAYOFF — 40s
NARRATION: Complaints dropped to near zero.
BEAT: The complaint counter from the cold open reappears — and falls off a cliff to zero. Hold on the zero. Silence.
NARRATION (cont.): Nothing got faster. The total journey was the same or slightly worse. They simply converted empty waiting into occupied walking, and the experience of the wait collapsed.
BEAT: The two eight-minute bars sit side by side — identical length, one mostly empty, one mostly occupied. Only the empty one is drawn in the harsh accent.
OPEN LOOP: If perception is this easy to move, what else is being moved?

## [03:09] FAIRNESS BEAT — 38s
NARRATION: Which raises the obvious objection: isn't this just manipulation? You're not being served faster. You're being *managed*.
BEAT: Two paths split from the carousel — one to a checkmark, one to an X.
NARRATION (cont.): Sometimes, yes. A progress bar that lies about progress is a lie, and a made-up "your call is important to us" queue position is worse than silence. But a wait with a visible end, a stated reason, and something to do is genuinely a better experience — not a trick, just a design that accounts for the fact that a person is standing there.
BEAT: Two progress bars: one jumps to 90% then freezes; the other moves honestly and finishes. The honest one gets the accent.
NARRATION (cont.): The line is whether the design respects the wait or disguises it.
BEAT: Text "RESPECT vs DISGUISE" draws, split by a hard vertical rule.
OPEN LOOP: What does that give you, standing in a queue tomorrow?

## [03:47] PAYOFF SETUP + CLOSE — 46s
NARRATION: Two useful things. The first is diagnostic. When a wait feels unbearable, check whether it's actually long or merely empty — because those have completely different fixes, and you've probably been applying the wrong one.
BEAT: Two toolkits draw. One labelled for LONG, one for EMPTY. The EMPTY one lights up.
NARRATION (cont.): The second is that you can do to yourself what the airport did to its passengers. Not distraction for its own sake — occupation. Deciding in advance what the wait is for turns dead time into something with a shape.
BEAT: A blank waiting bar fills with a single chosen activity, drawn deliberately, not scattered.
NARRATION (cont.): And when a company makes you wait in a blank room with no information and no estimate, now you know that's a choice. Every one of those is fixable, cheaply, and they've decided not to.
BEAT: A blank waiting room draws — bare walls, no clock, no board. Then a clock, a board, and an estimate are added one by one, each landing with weight.
NARRATION (cont.): The wait was never the problem. It was the emptiness inside it.
BEAT: Final wide shot — camera zooms out: carousel, corridor, queue, the two bars, the counter at zero, all one connected page. Accent underline draws beneath.
CLOSE: Loop closes.

## Sources used inline
- Stone, A. (2012). "Why Waiting Is Torture." *The New York Times*, Aug 18 —
  the Houston airport gate-relocation account (1 min walk / 7 min wait →
  6 min walk / 2 min wait; complaints to near zero)
- Maister, D. H. (1985). "The Psychology of Waiting Lines" — occupied vs.
  unoccupied time; uncertain and unexplained waits feel longer; unfair
  waits feel longest
- Larson, R. C. (1987). "Perspectives on Queues: Social Justice and the
  Psychology of Queueing." *Operations Research*, 35(6) — queue fairness,
  the single-serpentine-line result
- Note: the Houston figures are as reported by the NYT, not a peer-reviewed
  measurement. Frame as "the airport found", never as a controlled study.
