# Script — supermarket-psychology
Target runtime: ~9:45 | Target WPM: 155-165 (voice-style.md)

## [00:00] COLD OPEN
NARRATION: You walked in for milk. Twenty minutes later you're at the checkout with milk, plus seven things you didn't know existed when you woke up this morning.
BEAT: A shopping cart is drawn, then several small item icons pile into it one by one.
NARRATION (cont.): That wasn't an accident, and it wasn't really about you having weak willpower either. You walked through a space that has been measured, mapped, and adjusted — long before you ever pushed the cart through the door.
BEAT: A wall of shelves is drawn behind the cart, faint grid lines.

## [00:25] PROMISE
NARRATION: By the end of this video, you'll be able to see the actual structure underneath a store you've walked through a thousand times — and you won't be able to un-see it.
BEAT: Text stamps: "YOU WON'T UN-SEE THIS."

## [00:48] SETUP
NARRATION: Start at the door. The first several feet of almost every supermarket are strangely empty — a little too much open space for how expensive retail real estate actually is.
BEAT: An entrance archway is drawn, with unusually empty floor space in front of it.
NARRATION (cont.): Researchers who study retail spaces have a name for this: the decompression zone. You're still adjusting from outside pace to inside pace, and stores have learned that people barely register displays placed right at the entrance.
BEAT: A person icon is drawn, walking through the empty zone, unfocused.

## [01:40] FIRST EXPLANATION
NARRATION: So the obvious explanation for a store's layout is: it's just wherever things happened to fit. Convenient logistics, nothing more.
BEAT: A shelf is drawn plainly, no emphasis.
NARRATION (cont.): There's truth in that — refrigeration lines, delivery access, and storage all genuinely shape a layout. But that explanation quietly falls apart the moment you look at where the milk actually is.
BEAT: A shelf icon is drawn again, and an arrow-down icon points toward the far back corner of the frame.

## [02:35] TURN
NARRATION: In most supermarkets, milk, eggs, and bread — the things people come in for most often — sit at the back, or scattered along the far walls. Almost never near the door.
BEAT: The cart from earlier is redrawn, an arrow tracing a long path across the frame toward a shelf in the back.
NARRATION (cont.): Walk that path enough times, in enough stores, and it stops looking accidental. It looks like a route designed to maximize how many other shelves stand between you and the thing you actually came for.
BEAT: Small item icons appear scattered along the traced path.

## [03:35] TURN (continued)
NARRATION: And this part isn't just a theory — researchers have actually tracked real shopping trips, using in-store location data, and mapped the paths people walk. They found the paths weren't random at all. They fell into distinct, predictable patterns — and those patterns predict how much of the store, and how many products, a given shopper ends up exposed to.
BEAT: Several faint path-lines are drawn across the store map, converging into recognizable shapes.
NARRATION (cont.): Your walking route through a grocery store has been measured closely enough to model mathematically. Sit with that for a second.
BEAT: Camera zooms out to reveal the full mapped store with all paths visible at once.

## [05:00] DEEPER CAUSE
NARRATION: Here's the deeper mechanism, though — it's not just about the path. It's about what's at eye level along the way.
BEAT: A shelf is drawn with three horizontal rows.
NARRATION (cont.): Retailers have a blunt phrase for it: eye level is buy level. And it's not just an industry saying — controlled experiments that physically moved products up or down a shelf, without changing the product itself, found real, measurable shifts in sales just from the height of the shelf.
BEAT: An arrow-up icon moves a small item icon from a low shelf row to the middle row; a checkmark appears.

## [06:10] DEEPER CAUSE (continued)
NARRATION: Which means the position of nearly everything you almost bought today — not just where it sits in the store, but where it sits on the shelf in front of you — was tested, adjusted, and chosen. Usually by someone who has never met you, optimizing for shoppers in general.
BEAT: Brain icon is drawn beside the shelf, a small question-mark fading beside it.
NARRATION (cont.): There's a term for the broader effect of all of this working together — when a space is disorienting enough, in just the right way, that you lose track of why you walked in at all. It's called the Gruen transfer, named, a little ironically, after the architect who designed the first modern shopping mall — and who later publicly regretted what his own designs were used for.
BEAT: A wall/maze icon is drawn, with a small person icon inside it, turning in circles.

## [07:30] PAYOFF SETUP
NARRATION: None of this means every part of a store is a trap, or that shopping is some sinister ritual. Refrigeration needs are real. Delivery logistics are real. Not every choice is about you.
BEAT: A checkmark and an x-mark are drawn side by side, neutral.
NARRATION (cont.): But knowing the structure changes how you move through it. You don't need a conspiracy theory. You just need to notice the decompression zone, notice the walk to the back wall, notice what's sitting conveniently at eye level.
BEAT: The earlier entrance, path, and shelf icons are redrawn small, in a row.

## [08:20] PAYOFF
NARRATION: A genuinely useful habit, if you want one: write your list in the order you'll actually walk the store, not the order items come to mind. It sounds almost too simple. It also means you spend less time inside the parts of the layout built to slow you down and expose you to more.
BEAT: A calendar-like list is drawn with a checkmark ticking down it.
NARRATION (cont.): You still get to buy the seven extra things, if you actually want them. The point was never to shop less. It's to notice the moment the store started deciding for you — and hand that decision back to yourself.
BEAT: Final wide shot — camera zooms out to reveal cart, entrance, path map, and shelf all together on the page — accent-colored underline draws beneath the closing frame.
